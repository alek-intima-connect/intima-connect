/// Our Doctrine
The Great Expansion

We are told that socialism is a foreign concept — yet we live with it every day. The fire department doesn't ask for your credit card. The highway has no toll at every mile. The library is free. These systems work because they prioritize human need over private profit. Our doctrine is simple: expand what works.

We must take our money back from the bourgeoisie — the parasitic class that hoards the surplus value of our labor — and vector it into expanding these systems. Healthcare, housing, and food should function like the fire department: available to all, funded by the wealth we created, and free at the point of use.

The Canon is not a reading list. It is a record of everything the powerful hoped you'd never find.

/// Why this matters now

The world's eight richest individuals hold as much wealth as the bottom half of humanity combined. Not because they worked harder — because the systems we inherited were designed to concentrate value at the top and keep it there. The Canon exists to name that design, explain how it works, and document every time in history people have successfully built something better. If you've ever felt like the economy isn't working for you despite doing everything right — you're not wrong. This is the vocabulary for what you've already experienced.

Core concepts — start here
Labor Theory of Value
Where does profit come from?
When you work, you produce more value than your paycheck reflects. That gap — between what you produce and what you're paid — is called surplus value. It flows upward to shareholders and owners. This isn't an accident; it's the mechanism the system runs on.
Collective Ownership
Who should own the means of production?
Factories, hospitals, and housing are built by workers — yet owned by investors. Collective ownership means the people who operate a resource share in its control and returns. Worker co-ops, municipal utilities, and public land trusts are working examples right now.
Historical Examples
Has this worked before?
The Paris Commune (1871), the New Deal (1933–39), and the Nordic social democracies all demonstrate that redistributive, worker-centered policy produces measurable gains in health, literacy, and life expectancy — and that the ruling class fights hard to end them.
Modern Alternatives
What could replace it?
Universal Basic Income decouples survival from employment. Worker co-operatives put governance in employees' hands. Municipal ownership removes profit motive from essential services. None of these are theoretical — all exist and function today.
